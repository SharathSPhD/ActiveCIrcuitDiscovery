\subsection{IOI Circuit Recovery}

We first evaluate intervention-selection quality on the Indirect Object
Identification (IOI) task: 5 prompts with a budget of $B=20$
feature-level interventions per prompt.  The fair comparison fixes the
intervention type to \emph{ablation} for every method, so that all
strategies are scored on the same ablation-KL scale against an
ablation oracle that greedily ranks the candidate set by true ablation
KL (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).  Under this protocol the agent is the
ablation-constrained variant \emph{POMDP-abl}, which uses the full
Expected Free Energy (EFE) objective to choose \emph{which} feature to
test but is denied the steering action.  The multi-action agent that
also chooses the intervention \emph{type} is analysed separately in
Section~\ref{sec:rck}.  We report mean and median per-step KL and the
\emph{bounded oracle efficiency} (cumulative method KL divided by
cumulative oracle KL, $\leq 100\%$ by construction) with bootstrap
95\% confidence intervals over prompts (Table~\ref{tab:ioi}).

\subsection{Multi-Action Amplification: Relative Cumulative KL}
\label{sec:rck}

When the agent is allowed to choose the intervention \emph{type}, it
overwhelmingly selects feature steering, which scales a feature's
activation rather than zeroing it and can therefore produce KL
divergences far larger than ablation on the same feature.  Comparing
this multi-action cumulative KL to the \emph{ablation} oracle yields
ratios well above 100\%, so it is \emph{not} an oracle efficiency.  We
report it under a distinct name, \textbf{Relative Cumulative KL
(RCK)}: the ratio of a multi-action method's cumulative KL to the
cumulative KL of the ablation oracle (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).  To
isolate how much of this amplification comes from \emph{selection}
versus simply \emph{having the steering action}, we compare the full
POMDP against action-matched baselines that share the identical
multi-action space---greedy+steer, bandit+steer, random+steer---and a
pure random-action control (Table~\ref{tab:rck}).

\subsection{Feature Steering and Causal Controllability}

We test causal controllability by scaling individual transcoder
feature activations across a multiplier sweep
$m \in \{0, 1.5, 2, 3, 5, 10\}$ on 5 concept prompts with 10
circuit-selected features each (50 features per model).  To separate
genuine selectivity from out-of-distribution (OOD) effects of large
multipliers, we add a matched \emph{random-feature control}: 10 random
active features per prompt subjected to the identical sweep.  We
record, at each multiplier, the number of top-token prediction changes
and the mean KL divergence for selected and control features
(Table~\ref{tab:steering}).

Two effects are visible.  First, \emph{manipulability} (H2a): both KL
and prediction-change rate rise monotonically with the multiplier, and
at $m{=}10$ the selected-feature change rate (11/50 on Gemma, 9/50 on
Llama) is far above the per-token chance rate (a one-sided binomial
test against chance gives $p<10^{-8}$).  Steering transcoder features
does causally and dose-dependently move the output distribution.
Second, and more cautiously, \emph{selectivity} (H2b): circuit-selected
features carry higher mean KL than random controls at large
multipliers (e.g.\ $0.367$ vs.\ $0.086$ on Gemma at $m{=}10$), but the
\emph{prediction-change} advantage is small (11 vs.\ 8 on Gemma, 9
vs.\ 6 on Llama).  A one-sided Fisher exact test on the selected-vs-control
change counts is not significant, either at $m{=}10$ or pooled over
$m\geq 2$.  We therefore report that circuit-selected features are only
\emph{marginally}, and not significantly, more controllable than random
active features at the prediction level, and that the largest
multipliers push activations off-distribution---so the steering result
should be read as a controllability demonstration, not as proof of
privileged selectivity.

\subsection{Active Discovery Dynamics}
\label{sec:dynamics}

The POMDP agent maintains explicit beliefs over three hidden state
factors and updates them after each intervention.  Across IOI prompts,
total belief entropy decreases over the budget, indicating information
accumulation about circuit structure.

The agent's action selection differs sharply between architectures,
which explains the RCK contrast in Section~\ref{sec:rck}.  On Gemma
IOI, ablation is selected first for exploration, after which the agent
transitions to predominantly feature steering (94/100 steps; 94/95
post-first-step) with occasional activation patching (1/100).  On
Llama IOI the agent is far more conservative, choosing ablation for
70/100 steps and steering only 28/100---so its cumulative KL stays
near the ablation scale rather than amplifying.  The step-by-step
action distribution for both models is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:action_dist}.  This action-policy divergence, rather
than any difference in feature ranking, is what drives the very
different RCK values across the two models.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\input{figures/action_distribution}
\caption{Action type distribution across intervention steps for Gemma-2-2B (top) and Llama-3.2-1B (bottom).}
\label{fig:action_dist}
\end{figure*}

The attribution analysis reveals several structural properties.
Gemma-2-2B activates approximately 12\,600 transcoder features per
IOI prompt, of which roughly 2\,200 survive pruning at the 80\%
influence threshold; Llama-3.2-1B activates approximately 8\,000,
with roughly 1\,600 surviving.  On Gemma, causally important features
span all 26 layers but concentrate in layers 0--6 and 24--25, whereas
on Llama the distribution is more compressed, with early layers
(0--4) dominant.  By feature count, early-layer features (0--8)
form the plurality of the top-10, while the single highest-impact
features by KL are consistently found in layers 24--25.
Attribution graph generation takes approximately
18\,s on Gemma and 12\,s on Llama; each
\texttt{feature\_intervention} call takes approximately 0.03\,s.

Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

every Llama condition---so we describe the agent as \emph{competitive
with at best, and on Llama often behind}, the action-matched baselines
on the shallower architecture.

The four pre-registered hypotheses (H1--H4) are evaluated against the
experimental evidence and summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:hypothesis_eval}.  H2 is split into manipulability
(H2a) and selectivity (H2b) following the steering controls above.

\textbf{H1 (Efficiency).}  Under the fair ablation-only protocol,
POMDP-abl significantly beats random on Gemma IOI ($+43.5\%$,
paired permutation $p=0.031$, $n=5$) but not on Llama IOI ($+7.8\%$,
$p=0.41$).  \emph{Verdict:} accepted for Gemma; not accepted for Llama.
We make no claim of beating EAP, which is the strongest ablation
baseline throughout.

\textbf{H2 (Causal controllability).}  H2a (manipulability) is
supported: steering produces dose-dependent prediction changes far
above chance ($p<10^{-8}$ at $m{=}10$).  H2b (selectivity) is
\emph{not} supported: circuit-selected features are only marginally,
and not significantly (Fisher exact, n.s.), more likely to change
predictions than random active features, and the largest multipliers
are off-distribution.  \emph{Verdict:} manipulability accepted,
selectivity not established.

\textbf{H3 (Discovery quality).}  Bounded oracle efficiency exceeds the
50\% criterion on Gemma IOI (82.0\%), Gemma multi-step (73.0\%), Llama
IOI (52.1\%) and all domain tasks, but \emph{fails} on Llama multi-step
(9.3\%, partially remedied to 37.8\% with finer bins).
\emph{Verdict:} accepted except for Llama multi-step.

\textbf{H4 (Cross-architecture transfer).}  Findings replicate fully on
Gemma and partially on Llama, where the agent is competitive on IOI and
domain tasks but the layer-role discretisation degrades multi-step
performance.  \emph{Verdict:} partially accepted.

Limitations of the present work are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.
